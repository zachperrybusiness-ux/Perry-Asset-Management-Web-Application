Walk-Forward Backtest (Out-of-Sample Validation) ?

Tests the MLR model's out-of-sample performance by training on an expanding window and testing on the next period, repeatedly. Standard backtests suffer from in-sample overfitting — a model that looks great when evaluated on the data it was trained on may perform poorly on new data. Walk-forward estimates realistic forward performance by never testing on data the model has seen.

Enter a ticker and click "Run Walk-Forward Test" to see out-of-sample predictive performance.
Method: Expanding-window walk-forward validation (White 2000, Pesaran & Timmermann 1995) • Features: 21-day momentum, 63-day volatility, 10-day mean reversion, trailing return, VIX level, beta, RSI14 proxy • OLS regression trained on history up to each fold boundary • Strategy rule: enter long when model's predicted 21-day return exceeds threshold, hold for prediction horizon, exit • Compared against buy-and-hold baseline • No look-ahead bias — every prediction uses only data available at that point in time.

4 · Jump Detection Model ?

Simulates extreme return events with a stochastic jump intensity λ(t) following a CIR-style diffusion: dλ = κ(θ − λ)dt + σλ√λ dW

Load data, set parameters, then run simulation. Uses the Primary Asset.
Why a Doubly Stochastic Poisson (Cox) Process? ▼

1. Standard GBM is empirically false. It assumes constant volatility and no sudden discontinuous moves — real returns exhibit fat tails and jumps (Cont & Tankov, 2004).

2. Merton (1976) jump-diffusion adds a fixed-intensity Poisson process: dS/S = (μ − λk̄)dt + σdW + (eJ−1)dN(t). But real markets show clustering of crashes — the 2008 crisis, COVID, and 2022 inflation shock all featured jump arrivals in close succession.

3. The Cox process (Cox, 1955) allows λ itself to be stochastic. Here λ follows a CIR diffusion (Cox, Ingersoll & Ross, 1985), which guarantees non-negativity via the √λ term. This captures the empirical reality: crash risk is higher in some regimes than others.

4. Applications: credit default modeling (Duffie & Singleton, 1999), catastrophe bond pricing, high-frequency jump detection, stress testing.

Parameter guide: Higher κ = faster mean reversion in λ. Higher θ = more frequent jumps on average. Higher σλ = more regime shifts. Negative μJ biases jumps downward (crash risk). Larger σJ = more extreme jump magnitudes.

Sources: Cox, D.R. (1955) JRSS B · Merton, R.C. (1976) J. Financial Economics · Cont, R. & Tankov, P. (2004) Financial Modelling with Jump Processes, Chapman & Hall · Duffie, D. & Singleton, K. (1999) Review of Financial Studies · Cox, J.C., Ingersoll, J.E. & Ross, S.A. (1985) Econometrica.

7 · Value at Risk (VaR) ?

1-day and 10-day VaR at 95% / 99% confidence for the Primary Asset. Parametric, Historical, and Cornish-Fisher (fat-tail adjusted) methods.

Awaiting data.

Parametric VaR assumes normal returns — a known weakness given empirical fat tails. Historical simulation makes no distributional assumption but is limited by sample window. The Cornish-Fisher expansion (Cornish & Fisher, 1937) adjusts z-scores for skewness and excess kurtosis: zCF = z + (z²−1)S/6 + (z³−3z)K/24 − (2z³−5z)S²/36. CVaR (Expected Shortfall) answers: given that we are in the tail, what is the expected loss? CVaR is coherent and sub-additive; plain VaR is not (Artzner et al., 1999).

Sources: J.P. Morgan RiskMetrics Technical Document (1994) · Rockafellar, R.T. & Uryasev, S. (2000) "Optimization of Conditional Value-at-Risk," J. Risk · Cornish, E.A. & Fisher, R.A. (1937) Revue de l'Institut International de Statistique · Artzner, P., Delbaen, F., Eber, J.-M. & Heath, D. (1999) "Coherent Measures of Risk," Mathematical Finance.
